## LAUNCH POST — pin this

> Indiana nursing homes rank 50th in the country for staffing.
>
> Not in the bottom ten. Fiftieth — last, out of all fifty states and D.C. — for total
> nurse staffing hours per resident, once you account for how sick the residents are.
>
> That's not because the money isn't there. Over about fifteen years, Indiana nursing
> homes generated roughly $5.6 billion in Medicaid payments through county hospital
> ownership. Twenty-one county hospitals kept about 46% of it — more than $2.6 billion —
> and spent it on hospital buildings, equipment, and ambulances.
>
> The money came in for nursing home care. It left before it got to the floor.
>
> I'm a CNA. I've worked in Indiana long-term care for over ten years. Together Network
> is a project for the people who are still in the building — CNAs, QMAs, nurses, dietary,
> housekeeping, laundry — and for the residents and families depending on them.
>
> Staff safety and resident safety are the same problem. You can't ask someone to protect
> a resident's rights if protecting those rights gets them fired.
>
> This page will post what's actually documented: where the money goes, what the staffing
> numbers really are, and what your rights are. No fundraising, no outrage, no naming
> individual facilities.
>
> If you work in Indiana long-term care and want to talk privately, the link is in the
> first comment. Use your personal phone and personal email, not the facility's.
>
> Sources in comments.

## SERIES: THE MATH

### Post — the flagship

> Let's do the math out loud.
>
> Twenty residents. One aide. Eight hours.
>
> 480 minutes total. Subtract report at the start and end. Subtract charting. Subtract
> two meal passes. Subtract the time you spend looking for the one working Hoyer lift on
> the floor.
>
> You're left with somewhere under 20 minutes per resident, for the entire shift.
>
> In those 20 minutes: two rounds of incontinence care. Repositioning. A shower if it's
> their day. Getting them up. Feeding, if they need help. Answering the call light. Being
> a human being to someone who may not have had a visitor in three weeks.
>
> Twenty minutes.
>
> Nobody is failing at this job. The job is arithmetically impossible, and it was made
> that way on a spreadsheet by someone who has never done it.

### Post — the two-person transfer

> Every care plan in the building says "two-person assist."
>
> There is one person on the floor.
>
> So you either do it alone and risk your back and their hip, or you don't do it and
> they sit in it until someone comes. Those are the two options. The third option — the
> second person — was removed at the corporate level.
>
> Then when someone gets hurt, the incident report asks what the aide did wrong.

### Post — night shift

> Third shift, 40 residents, one aide, one nurse.
>
> Rounds every two hours. Forty people. Do that math and it's three minutes a person,
> assuming nobody falls, nobody needs a full change, nobody is having a bad night, and
> nobody is dying.
>
> Someone always is.
>
> Indiana averages 0.66 RN hours per resident per day. That's the whole state.

## SERIES: WHERE THE MONEY WENT

### Post — the mechanism

> Here's the part most people don't know.
>
> Over the last fifteen years or so, county-owned hospitals bought up nearly all of
> Indiana's roughly 500 nursing homes. Not because a county hospital in one town needed a
> nursing home four counties away — but because government ownership qualified those homes
> for a much higher Medicaid reimbursement rate. Around 36% higher than a privately owned
> facility gets.
>
> The extra money was justified as improving care for residents.
>
> The IndyStar spent years tracking where it went. Twenty-one county hospitals kept about
> 46% of the $5.6 billion those nursing homes generated. More than $2.6 billion.
>
> Meanwhile Indiana's nursing homes went to 50th in the nation for staffing.
>
> The money worked exactly as designed. It just wasn't designed for the residents.

### Post — the lawsuit detail

> When the IndyStar asked county hospitals how they were spending Medicaid money
> generated by nursing homes, eight of them wouldn't say.
>
> The paper had to sue in 2022 to get public records about public money.
>
> Ask yourself why numbers that should be routine required a lawsuit.

### Post — the buildings

> What the diverted nursing home money bought, according to the reporting: new hospital
> buildings. Renovations. Equipment. Ambulances. Technology.
>
> What it didn't buy: a second aide on the hall.
>
> Every one of those purchases probably looked reasonable in a board meeting. That's how
> two and a half billion dollars leaves a system without anyone deciding to harm anyone.

## SERIES: YOU'RE ALLOWED TO

### Post — §7 rights

> Things Indiana long-term care workers are allowed to do, that people are regularly told
> they can't:
>
> You're allowed to talk to your coworkers about staffing.
> You're allowed to talk to your coworkers about your pay.
> You're allowed to talk about scheduling, safety, and working conditions.
> You're allowed to do that together, as a group.
>
> Federal labor law protects most private-sector employees acting together over working
> conditions, whether or not there's a union anywhere near your building.
>
> Protected doesn't mean nobody will retaliate. It means retaliation is illegal and you
> can file on it. Which is why you document everything, with dates, on your own phone,
> off the clock.
>
> General information, not legal advice.

### Post — the 30-day rule

> If you're going to save one thing from this page, save this.
>
> If you're fired, demoted, cut, or written up after reporting a safety problem, you have
> **30 calendar days** to file a retaliation complaint with the Indiana Department of
> Labor. Thirty days. Not 30 business days.
>
> Most people lose that window without knowing it existed, because they spend the first
> month going through HR and internal appeals. The clock does not stop for that.
>
> File first. Appeal internally at the same time if you want. But file.
>
> in.gov/dol/iosha/whistleblower-protection-unit — general information, not legal advice.

### Post — anonymous complaints

> You can file a complaint with the Indiana Department of Health anonymously.
> 1-800-246-8909, or online.
>
> You can also contact the Long-Term Care Ombudsman, and their office does not release a
> complainant's name without permission.
>
> Two honest caveats. Anonymous doesn't always mean unidentifiable — if you're the only
> one who was there, the facility can often work it out. And a complaint only goes
> anywhere if it's specific: date, time, shift, who, what happened, what the outcome was,
> where the resident is now.
>
> Vague complaints get closed. Specific ones get surveyed.

## FAMILY-FACING POSTS

### Post — questions to ask

> If you have someone in an Indiana nursing home, here are five questions worth asking,
> in writing, and keeping the answer to:
>
> 1. How many aides are assigned to my parent's hall on each shift?
> 2. What was that number last weekend, and overnight?
> 3. How many of the aides working today are agency staff who've never met them?
> 4. What's the turnover rate on this unit this year?
> 5. Who do I contact if the answer to any of these changes?
>
> Ask by email so there's a record. A facility that staffs well answers these easily.
>
> Staffing data is also public on Medicare's Care Compare site. You don't have to take
> anyone's word for it.

### Post — what to watch for

> Things families notice that usually mean short staffing, not bad aides:
>
> Call lights ringing a long time. Someone still in bed at 11am who used to get up.
> Weight loss. New skin breakdown. Being wet when you arrive, consistently, at the same
> time of day. A completely new face every visit.
>
> The aide standing in front of you probably knows all of it and can't say it. She is
> likely carrying 15 to 20 people and has been told to move faster.
>
> Direct the question up. Ask the administrator, in writing, how many staff were assigned
> to that hall.

### Post — the alliance

> To families: the aide caring for your mother is on your side. She sees what you see and
> she's not allowed to say it.
>
> When you ask hard questions about staffing, you're asking them on behalf of someone who
> would be fired for asking. That's not an exaggeration; it's the most common story I
> hear.
>
> Families can say out loud what workers can't. That's the most useful thing you have.

## ACTION POSTS

### Post — the federal floor is gone

> Something that got very little coverage in Indiana and should have gotten a lot.
>
> In 2024 the federal government set a minimum staffing standard for nursing homes —
> 3.48 total nurse hours per resident per day, and a registered nurse in the building 24
> hours a day.
>
> A federal court struck it down in April 2025. Then Congress blocked it from being
> enforced until after September 30, 2034. In February 2026 the rule was formally
> repealed.
>
> Indiana has never had a state minimum of its own.
>
> So as of right now, there is no floor. None. For at least the next eight years, whatever
> Indiana decides is the only thing that exists.
>
> That's not a reason to give up. It's the entire reason state law is now the only thing
> that matters.

### Post — the 2027 window

> The Indiana General Assembly's 2027 session is the long one — the budget session. It's
> where Medicaid reimbursement for nursing homes actually gets decided.
>
> Between now and January, legislators are in the interim. They're doing study committees.
> Almost nobody is contacting them about long-term care right now, which means right now
> is when a message actually lands.
>
> Find your legislator at iga.in.gov. It takes about four minutes.
>
> Three things worth asking for:
> 1. A state minimum staffing standard, since the federal one is gone until 2034.
> 2. A requirement that Medicaid money generated by a nursing home be spent on that
>    nursing home's direct care, reported publicly by facility.
> 3. Protection from retaliation for caregivers who report unsafe conditions.
>
> If you'd rather not use your name, families can send this. Workers should think hard
> before signing something on facility letterhead territory.
