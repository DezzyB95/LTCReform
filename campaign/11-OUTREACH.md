# Outreach — DMs, Replies, Reporters, Allies

Social media is the top of the funnel. This file is the rest of it. A campaign that posts
well and converses badly gets attention and no contacts.

---

## 1. Replying in comments

**When a worker says "this is my building too":**
> That's what I keep hearing, from all over the state. You're not imagining it and you're
> not the problem.

Then stop. Don't pitch, don't DM them. Let them come to you. A worker who is approached
gets scared; a worker who approaches you is already committed.

**When someone asks what they should do:**
> Depends what you're dealing with. Two things that apply to almost everyone: write
> everything down with dates on your own phone, and know that if something happens to you
> after you report a safety issue, the window to file with the state is 30 calendar days.
> Happy to talk through the rest privately if that's useful.

**When someone tells a long, painful story publicly:**
> Thank you for putting that in writing. That's more useful than you know. If you're
> comfortable, message me — and if you're still working there, don't add any more detail
> in a public comment.

**When someone attacks you as bitter:**
> Fair thing to wonder. Indiana is 50th out of fifty for staffing and $2.6 billion went to
> hospital buildings instead of nursing homes. Those numbers were there before me.

Once. Never twice. Never a second reply to the same person.

**When an administrator or DON pushes back with substance:**
> I'd honestly rather hear from someone running a building than not. Where do you think
> the number is wrong?

Some of your best information will come from managers who agree with you and can't say so.

## 2. The first DM (after they message you)

Do not send a form letter. Send this, adapted:

> Hey — thanks for reaching out. I'm a CNA, I've been in Indiana LTC for over ten years,
> and this project is mostly me so far.
>
> Two things first. Use your personal phone and personal email for this, not anything
> connected to work. And don't send me photos from inside the building or anything with
> resident information in it — I can't use it and it can cost you your license.
>
> You don't have to tell me anything specific if you're not ready. Mostly I'm trying to
> understand what's actually happening across the state: how many residents people are
> being assigned, what happens when someone raises a safety concern, whether anyone's
> gotten anywhere with the state.
>
> Whatever you tell me stays private. I don't publish anything without asking you first,
> in writing.
>
> What's your county, roughly, and what shift do you work?

Then **listen**. Don't recruit on the first exchange. The ask comes on the second or third.

## 3. The ask (second or third conversation)

> Two things that would genuinely help, if you're up for either:
>
> 1. Would you be willing to be a quiet contact for your county? Nothing public, name goes
>    nowhere. It just means if another CNA around you is dealing with something, there's
>    one person nearby who knows how the process works.
>
> 2. If you ever leave that job, would you be willing to say some of this publicly? Former
>    staff can say what current staff can't, and reporters need people on record.
>
> Either way, no pressure at all, and nothing changes if the answer's no.

## 4. Reporter outreach

Indiana LTC reporting is a live beat — the IndyStar's "Careless" series is ongoing work,
and the Indiana Capital Chronicle, WRTV, WFYI, Side Effects Public Media, and Skilled
Nursing News all cover this. Reporters need frontline sources and rarely have them,
because frontline workers can't talk.

**Cold email template:**

> Subject: CNA source on Indiana LTC staffing — 50th in the nation
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I read your [specific piece] on [specific thing]. I'm a CNA with over ten years in
> Indiana long-term care, and I run a small project collecting what Indiana LTC workers
> are actually experiencing on staffing and retaliation.
>
> Two things you might find useful:
>
> One, I'm hearing consistent reports of assignments in the 15–20 resident range on day
> shift and 30–40 overnight, statewide, which lines up with Indiana ranking 50th on
> acuity-adjusted staffing hours — 3.51 total nurse HPRD against a national average of
> 3.76 — and staffing 29.6% below residents' expected need in Q1 2026 federal data.
>
> Two, an angle I don't think has been covered: resident-rights enforcement in Indiana runs
> entirely through the most retaliation-exposed person in the building. Aides get written
> up for honoring a resident's legal refusal of care. Indiana is at-will and the IOSHA
> retaliation window is 30 calendar days, which most workers miss doing internal appeals.
> That's why complaint volume understates the problem.
>
> I can talk on background any time, and I can connect you with current and former Indiana
> LTC staff. I'm careful about protecting people who are still working.
>
> [Name] — [phone] — [project link]

Send to three reporters, individually, never as a group. Follow up once after ten days,
then stop.

## 5. Allied organizations

Approach these as a peer with something to offer — frontline contacts — not as someone
asking for help. You already tried "please help me" with several of these and it didn't
convert. Offer instead.

| Organization | What you offer | What you want |
|---|---|---|
| **United Senior Action** (Indianapolis, 317-634-0872) | Frontline worker voices for their LTC policy work | Legislative access, coalition cover, a 2027 agenda slot |
| **AARP Indiana** (One N. Capitol Ave #1275, 866-448-3618) | The worker side of a story they only tell from the resident side | Amplification, their scorecard data, legislative reach |
| **Indiana Long-Term Care Ombudsman** (Mary Swinford, 317-232-7134) | A pipeline of specific, verifiable complaints in the format their office asked for | Their resident-rights training materials, and a standing relationship |
| **Consumer Voice** (national) | Indiana-specific data and worker contacts | National platform, their staffing analyses |
| **LTCCC / nursinghome411** | Ground truth behind their Indiana numbers | Their quarterly staffing data, credibility by association |
| **Emergency Workplace Organizing Committee** | Indiana LTC workers who want to organize | Free organizer training for you and your county contacts |
| **UFCW Local 700 / SEIU** | Warm contacts in Indiana LTC | What has and hasn't worked in Indiana, and their mistakes |
| **Indiana Capital Chronicle** | Frontline sourcing | Coverage |

**Template:**

> Subject: Indiana LTC frontline contacts — offering, not asking
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I'm a CNA with ten-plus years in Indiana long-term care. I run a small project that's
> building direct contact with frontline LTC workers around the state — the people whose
> experience shows up in your data but who almost never talk to anyone.
>
> I'm writing because I think I have something you don't: workers who will talk, in
> counties across the state, with a protocol for protecting them.
>
> Indiana is 50th nationally on acuity-adjusted staffing, staffs 29.6% below residents'
> expected need, and the federal minimum staffing standard is
> repealed and blocked from enforcement until after 2034. State-level work is the only
> thing left, and the 2027 budget session is the window.
>
> Is there someone there who works on long-term care I could talk to for twenty minutes?
>
> [Name] — [phone] — [project link]

## 6. Legislators

Interim study committee season is the cheap window. Legislators hear almost nothing about
LTC between sessions.

- Find yours at **iga.in.gov**. Email beats a form. A phone call to the office beats email.
- One page. Three asks. One local fact. One specific request ("will you support a state
  staffing standard in the 2027 session?").
- Ask family members to send it, not current workers.
- Track every response in a spreadsheet: name, district, party, date, response, follow-up.
  That list is the most valuable thing this campaign will produce.
- Identify the members of the interim committee handling health/human services and write
  to them regardless of district.

## 7. What to say when someone asks about your own situation

Publicly, and in DMs with people you don't know:

> I left long-term care after raising safety concerns. I'd rather keep the focus on the
> statewide numbers than on me — they're worse than my story and they're documented.

Then move on. See `09-LEGAL-SAFETY.md` for why this matters more than it feels like it
does.
