# Legal & Safety Guardrails

**Read this before the first post goes out.** These are not stylistic preferences.

Not legal advice. Run the litigation section past your counsel or, if you're proceeding
pro se, treat it as the conservative default until a lawyer tells you otherwise.

---

## 1. You have active litigation. That changes what you can safely post.

There is a live employment case against a former employer, with opposing counsel actively
filing. Assume the following, because all of it is true:

- **Opposing counsel will find these accounts.** Searching a plaintiff's social media is
  standard, cheap, and among the first things done.
- **Public posts are discoverable and admissible.** A post is a written statement by a
  party. It can be attached to a motion. It does not get context or tone.
- **Anything you say about the facts of your employment can be used to impeach you**
  if it differs by a word from your complaint, your deposition, or your discovery
  responses. Not because you lied — because memory is imprecise and the two versions will
  be put side by side.
- **Damages arguments read your posts too.** Content suggesting a thriving new venture can
  be used against a lost-income claim.

### The hard rules

| Never | Instead |
|---|---|
| Name the former employer or any of its facilities | "an Indiana facility," "the industry," or a public-record statewide figure |
| Describe your own termination, write-ups, or assignments as facts | "Workers report…" / "This is the pattern I hear constantly" |
| Post about filings, hearings, discovery, or opposing counsel | Nothing. Zero. Not even vague. |
| Characterize any company as having broken the law | Describe the *system*, cite public records |
| Post while angry, or within an hour of a case development | 24-hour hold on anything written upset |

### The safe frame

Speak as **an advocate describing a documented statewide pattern**, not as a plaintiff
describing her case. That framing is both legally safer and rhetorically stronger — "the
state is 50th" beats "this happened to me" with every audience except a jury.

You *can* say, generally and without detail, that you are a CNA who has worked in Indiana
long-term care for over a decade and left after raising safety concerns, and that the
experience is why this project exists. Keep it to a sentence in your bio. Don't relitigate
it in posts.

**When your case concludes, revisit this file.** Most of these restrictions lift, and your
story becomes the campaign's strongest asset. It just can't be that yet.

## 2. Defamation

Truth is a defense, but defending costs money you don't have. So:

- Post **public records and published journalism**, and link them. Sourced facts from the
  IndyStar or CMS are the safest content you can publish.
- Never accuse a named person or company of a crime, fraud, or abuse.
- Frame institutional criticism against the identifiable public record, never against a
  facility you personally worked in.
- Keep the source link with every statistic, in the post or in the first comment.

## 3. Other people's stories

When workers or families send you things:

- **Written permission before anything is published**, even anonymized. Get it in writing,
  in the same channel.
- Strip facility name, city if the facility is the only one in town, dates, shift, unit,
  job title if unusual, and every proper noun.
- Publish composites and patterns rather than individual accounts wherever possible.
- Never post an intake submission. Intake is confidential and it needs to stay that way or
  nobody will use it again.
- If someone describes ongoing resident harm, tell them how to report it (IDOH, Ombudsman,
  APS, 911) — do not turn it into content.

## 4. PHI and HIPAA

You're not a covered entity, but workers who send you things are, and a post that gets a
CNA fired for a HIPAA violation would end this project's credibility overnight.

- Never publish anything photographed inside a facility.
- Never publish resident names, initials, ages, room numbers, diagnoses, or incidents
  specific enough to identify someone.
- Put this in writing on the intake form and repeat it in the pinned post: **do not send
  photos taken at work, and do not send resident information.**

## 5. Protecting the workers who contact you

This determines whether the project works at all.

- Tell people to use **personal devices, personal email, personal networks** — never
  facility wifi, never a work phone, never a work email.
- Say plainly that following the page publicly is visible to their employer. Recommend
  they follow from a personal account, or not at all — reading without following works.
- Never publicly acknowledge that a specific person contacted you.
- Never post a screenshot of a DM, even cropped. Screenshots leak metadata and writing
  style.
- Keep confidential intake **structurally separate** from public content, exactly as your
  site plan already says.

**The 30-day fact belongs in every worker-facing conversation.** IOSHA retaliation
complaints must be filed within 30 calendar days of the adverse action. People burn that
window on internal appeals and lose the claim before they know it existed.

## 6. NLRA §7 — say it accurately, it's your best worker-facing content

Most private-sector nursing home employees, union or not, have a federally protected right
to act together over wages, staffing, scheduling, and safety. That is genuinely
protected activity and most CNAs have never been told it exists.

- Frame it as: *"Talking with coworkers about staffing and working conditions is protected
  activity under federal labor law."*
- Always add: *"Protected does not mean nobody will retaliate. It means retaliation is
  illegal and you can file. Document everything, with dates."*
- Do not promise anyone a specific legal outcome, and don't tell someone whether their
  particular situation is protected. Give the general rule and point them to a source.
- Supervisors are generally excluded from §7 protection. Charge nurses may or may not
  count. Don't guess for individuals.

## 7. Election and nonprofit posture

You're a volunteer project, not a 501(c)(3), so there's no IRS limit on what you can say.
But: **stay non-partisan in tone.** In Indiana the reform mechanism requires Republican
votes, and a page that reads as a Democratic operation will never get them. You can
personally volunteer with a county party — just keep it off this project's accounts.

## 8. Pre-publish checklist

Before every post:

1. Does it name your former employer or any facility? → cut it
2. Does it describe your own case? → cut it
3. Does every number have a source I can link? → add it
4. Could a resident be identified from this? → cut it
5. Did someone give written permission for their words? → get it
6. Would I be fine with opposing counsel reading this aloud? → if not, cut it
7. Am I angry right now? → schedule it for tomorrow instead
