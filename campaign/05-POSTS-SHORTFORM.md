# Instagram

Reels are reposted TikToks (see `04-POSTS-VIDEO.md`). What's unique to Instagram is the
**carousel** — the format people screenshot and send to a coworker. Carousels are the
single highest-value asset type in this campaign because they travel through group chats,
which is where workers actually talk.

Design: off-white background, dark charcoal text, one accent color, large type, no
photos, no stock imagery, no logos on every slide. See `08-GRAPHICS-BRIEFS.md`.

## Carousel 1 — "Where the money went" (6 slides)

1. **Indiana nursing homes rank 50th in the nation for staffing.**
2. Over ~15 years, county hospitals acquired nearly all of Indiana's ~500 nursing homes.
3. Government ownership qualified those homes for a Medicaid rate about 36% higher than
   privately owned facilities.
4. Those homes generated about **$5.6 billion**. Twenty-one county hospitals kept about
   **46%** of it — more than **$2.6 billion**.
5. It paid for hospital buildings, renovations, equipment, ambulances, technology.
6. **The money came in for nursing home care. It left before it reached the floor.**
   *Source: IndyStar "Careless" investigation.*

## Carousel 2 — "Your rights at work" (7 slides)

1. **Things Indiana LTC workers are allowed to do.**
2. Talk to coworkers about staffing.
3. Talk to coworkers about pay.
4. Talk about scheduling, safety, working conditions.
5. Do all of it together, as a group. Most private-sector workers are protected acting
   together over working conditions — union or not.
6. Protected doesn't mean nobody retaliates. It means retaliation is illegal and you can
   file.
7. **So document everything. Dates, times, names. Your phone. Off the clock.**
   *General information, not legal advice.*

## Carousel 3 — "30 days" (5 slides)

1. **If you're fired after reporting a safety problem, you have 30 days.**
2. Thirty **calendar** days to file a retaliation complaint with the Indiana Department
   of Labor.
3. Not 30 business days. Not 30 days after HR finishes reviewing.
4. Most people lose the window doing internal appeals first. The clock doesn't pause.
5. **File first. Appeal internally at the same time if you want.**
   in.gov/dol/iosha/whistleblower-protection-unit

## Carousel 4 — "For families: 5 questions" (7 slides)

1. **Five questions to ask an Indiana nursing home. In writing.**
2. How many aides are assigned to this hall, per shift?
3. What was that number last weekend? Overnight?
4. How many staff today are agency, and have they met my parent?
5. What's the turnover rate on this unit this year?
6. Who do I contact when that changes?
7. **Ask by email so there's a record. Staffing data is also public on Medicare Care
   Compare.**

## Carousel 5 — "How to file a complaint that actually goes somewhere" (7 slides)

1. **Vague complaints get closed. Specific ones get surveyed.**
2. **When:** date, time, shift.
3. **Who:** resident, staff, who witnessed it. (Don't publish this — put it in the
   complaint.)
4. **What:** specifically who said or did what.
5. **Outcome:** was the resident hurt, afraid, hospitalized, refused a choice?
6. **Where:** where it happened, where the resident is now.
7. **IDOH: 1-800-246-8909 or online. Ombudsman: in.gov/ombudsman. You can file
   anonymously.**

*Slides 2–6 follow the structure the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman's office gives for a
verifiable complaint. Using their own framework makes this hard to dismiss.*

## Carousel 6 — "There is no floor" (6 slides)

1. **There is no minimum staffing law for Indiana nursing homes right now.**
2. May 2024: CMS sets a federal minimum. 3.48 nursing hours per resident day. RN on site
   24/7.
3. April 2025: a federal court vacates it.
4. 2025: Congress bars enforcement until after September 30, 2034.
5. February 2026: formally repealed.
6. **Indiana never had its own. For at least eight years, state law is the only thing
   that can exist.**

# Bluesky / X

Reporters, legislators, policy staff, and researchers. Low volume, high signal. This is
where you post **receipts with links**, because reach doesn't matter here — being findable
and citable does.

## Thread 1 — the money (pin this)

> Indiana nursing homes rank 50th out of 50 states and DC for total nurse staffing hours
> per resident, adjusted for resident acuity.
>
> Not for lack of money. Here's where the money went. 🧵

> Over roughly 15 years, county-owned hospitals acquired nearly all of Indiana's ~500
> nursing homes. Government ownership qualified those homes for a Medicaid reimbursement
> rate about 36% higher than privately-owned facilities get.

> IndyStar's "Careless" investigation found 21 public hospitals kept about 46% of the
> $5.6B those nursing homes generated. More than $2.6 billion.

> It went to hospital buildings, renovations, equipment, ambulances, technology. Not to
> direct care staffing.

> When IndyStar asked how the money was spent, eight county hospitals refused. The paper
> sued in 2022 to obtain public records about public money.

> Meanwhile: Indiana staffs 29.6% below its own residents' expected need and ranks among
> the ten worst states (Q1 2026 federal data, LTCCC). Acuity-adjusted, Indiana provides
> 3.51 total nurse hours per resident day against a national average of 3.76.

> And there is now no federal backstop. The 2024 CMS minimum staffing rule was vacated in
> April 2025, barred from enforcement until after 9/30/2034 by statute, and formally
> repealed effective 2/2/2026.

> Indiana has never had a state minimum. So as of today there is no minimum staffing
> standard in Indiana nursing homes, and there won't be a federal one for at least eight
> years.
>
> The 2027 budget session is the only place this gets fixed.

## Thread 2 — the retaliation gap

> A thing Indiana nursing home policy consistently misses:
>
> Resident-rights education assumes someone can act on those rights without losing their
> job. In Indiana long-term care, that assumption is false. 🧵

> A resident has a federal right to refuse care. When an aide honors a refusal, the aide
> is frequently written up — because the facility's throughput target and the resident's
> right are in direct conflict, and only one of them has a disciplinary process attached.

> Indiana is at-will. There's no requirement that a facility show cause before terminating
> a frontline caregiver. The IOSHA retaliation window is 30 calendar days, which most
> workers blow on internal appeals before learning it exists.

> So the enforcement structure for resident rights runs through the single most
> retaliation-exposed person in the building, with no job protection and a one-month
> filing window.

> That's the gap. Any Indiana LTC reform that doesn't address caregiver retaliation
> protection is regulating a system whose only witnesses are structurally silenced.

# LinkedIn

Different register: professional, data-forward, no floor slang, no first-person grievance.
Audience is administrators, DONs, nurse leaders, agency owners, health policy people, and
your WGU human services network. Some of them are persuadable and a few of them are
sources.

Once a week. Longer form. Always end with a question that invites disagreement.

## Post 1

> Indiana ranks 50th in the nation for total nurse staffing hours per resident in nursing
> homes, adjusted for acuity. In the same period, Indiana nursing homes generated
> approximately $5.6 billion through county hospital ownership arrangements, of which
> roughly 46% was retained by 21 county hospitals — over $2.6 billion — and directed to
> hospital capital projects rather than resident care. (IndyStar, "Careless.")
>
> I don't think this is a story about bad actors. It's a story about an incentive
> structure that made a legal, board-approved decision look reasonable 500 times in a row.
>
> The mechanism is fixable. Require that supplemental Medicaid payments generated by a
> nursing home be spent on that nursing home's direct care, and report it publicly by
> facility. That's not a mandate on operators. It's a condition on money the public
> already spends.
>
> What's the strongest argument against facility-level spend-down reporting? I'd genuinely
> like to hear it.

## Post 2

> The federal minimum staffing standard for nursing homes is gone. Vacated by a federal
> court in April 2025, barred from enforcement by statute until after September 30, 2034,
> and formally repealed effective February 2, 2026.
>
> Indiana has never had a state standard.
>
> For operators, that removes a compliance deadline. It does not remove the underlying
> problem: Indiana staffs 29.6% below its own residents' assessed need and ranks 50th
> nationally on acuity-adjusted hours. Those numbers drive turnover, agency
> spend, survey deficiencies, and litigation exposure — all of which cost more than the
> staffing would have.
>
> The 2027 Indiana budget session is where this gets decided either way. Operators who
> want to shape a workable state standard have about four months to get in front of it,
> or they'll be reacting to one written by people who've never run a building.

## Post 3

> A structural observation from ten years as a CNA in Indiana long-term care.
>
> Resident rights are enforced, in practice, by the lowest-paid and most
> retaliation-exposed person in the building. A resident's right to refuse care only
> exists if an aide can honor that refusal without being disciplined for it. In an at-will
> state with a 30-day retaliation filing window, that's a weak enforcement mechanism.
>
> The practical implication for anyone running a facility: your incident reports and your
> grievance log are undercounting, and the size of that undercount is a function of how
> your staff expects you to react.
>
> If you want accurate safety data, the cheapest intervention available is a
> non-retaliation practice your staff actually believes.
